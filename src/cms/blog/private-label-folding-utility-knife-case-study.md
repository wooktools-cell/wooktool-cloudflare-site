If you already like the way a folding utility knife works, start by asking what you can change on that model. In one WOOKTOOL private-label project, the buyer started with an existing knife and sent a logo. We prepared artwork for the tool and packaging, and the project produced a finished branded knife and samples in several colors. For a buyer putting together a retail line, those are three separate approvals: what goes on the knife, what goes on the pack, and what the color looks like on the actual part.

## Decide which part of the knife should carry your brand

A logo file tells the factory what the mark looks like. It takes a placement drawing to show where it sits on the tool. In this project, the logo placement was worked out on the existing folding knife before the finished piece was shown. That gives the buyer something concrete to check: does the mark read clearly at its actual size, and is it on the side shoppers will see in the pack?

If you already have a model in mind, send its link or a photo with the preferred location marked. Include your original artwork if you have it. Ask the factory to show the logo on the product drawing before you request a sample. Where the mark goes and how it is applied can depend on the model and the surface available. ## Review the printed pack and the tool together

The project included separate print artwork and a mockup. That matters if you sell through a retailer or distributor with its own pack requirements. A knife can look right on its own while the pack shows the wrong variant, puts the barcode in an awkward spot, or hides the side that carries your logo.

Send the factory your preferred pack format, artwork or reference, and any text or barcode requirements for your market. Ask for a proof that shows how the knife will sit in the pack. If the same tool will be sold under several SKUs, check which product color, artwork file and barcode go together before approving the pack. ## Use physical samples to settle the color

The case shows several color samples on the existing knife design. That is a practical way to narrow down a choice when the color in a mockup looks different on the handle under ordinary lighting. Give the factory a color code or a physical reference if the shade matters, and record which sample you approve.

If you're testing demand, tell the factory which color you would order first and which you might add on a repeat order. Ask whether the minimum order quantity applies per color, per packaging version or to the full order. For example, a distributor might want one knife color for the first shipment and two printed packs for different channels. The split is worth discussing before comparing quotes.
